Case study

Shift Coverage: letting event staff cancel, offer up or swap a shift without leaving the manager out

A feature of the staff mobile app (iOS and Android) and the web admin of a SaaS platform that event agencies use to staff field events. The team had no UI/UX designer. I designed and built Shift Coverage end to end: the flows, the frontend, the backend and database, and the tests. I also wrote the feature overview for clients.

My roleUX flows and copy, frontend, backend and database, tests, client overview
StackVue 3, TypeScript, Pinia, Capacitor · PHP, PostgreSQL · Vitest, Playwright
UsersEvent staff on their phones; agency managers on the web
ConstraintA manager approves every change; nothing moves on the schedule without them

The problem

Staff who couldn't make a shift had no way to say so in the app. Some just didn't show up, and managers spent time phoning around for a replacement.

The first ask was shift swap. While designing it, I saw that a worker who can't come often has no one to trade with. So I proposed two more paths: offer the shift up to qualified coworkers, or ask to cancel it.

Cancel

The worker picks a reason and asks to come off the shift. They stay scheduled until the manager approves.

Offer up

Only coworkers in the same position, with no clashing shift, see it. One taps Apply, the manager approves, and the shift moves.

Swap

Pick a coworker, then one of their shifts. They see what they'd give up and get. Both shifts trade at once, or not at all.

Cancel a shift

Anna wakes up ill. She asks to come off Sunday's shift, but stays scheduled until her manager decides. An unanswered request never leaves the shift empty: it shows "Cancellation not answered" and she is still expected.

Offer up a shift

Ben can't make Saturday but would rather a coworker took it. Only coworkers in the same position, for the same client and with no clashing shift see the offer. They never see his reason.

Swap a shift

Ben would take Anna's shift if she takes his. The sheet only lists coworkers with a shift he could trade for. Both stay scheduled until Anna accepts and the manager approves; then both shifts trade in one step, or nothing changes.

One place for everything waiting on you

Home has two tabs: "My shifts" and "Requests". A red count shows what's waiting. Every card opens the full shift details with the staffer's own pay, so they can decide before saying yes. A passed-on offer can be re-applied for with one tap.

The manager's side

One list for every cancel, offer-up and swap request. Managers only see the programs, markets and customers they look after. Warnings surface what's stuck, "Approve selected" says why any were skipped, and every change lands in the shift's history in plain sentences.

The cost change, before Approve

People can earn different rates for the same shift, so approving a swap or an offer-up can change what the shift costs. Managers see who works it now and after, the hourly and total difference, and a plain-language total. Any change is highlighted; a missing rate says "unknown" instead of guessing. Staff never see anyone else's pay.

Alerts and push notifications

Coverage messages go through the alerts system clients already use. Each client chooses email, text or push notification for six moments, from "a shift was offered up" to "your request wasn't answered", and writes its own wording. Alerts respect each staffer's email and SMS choices and never include the reason. Push notifications open the right screen in the app; I was finishing them when the team was let go.

Privacy and safety

  • A manager approves every change. There is no way to switch that off.
  • Only qualified, active staff take part, and nobody gets double-booked.
  • Each client sets a time limit, so a cancellation can't land two hours before doors open.
  • Coworkers never see the reason, the manager's note or anyone else's pay. A refused swap only says "That shift is no longer available."
  • On a shared phone, signing out wipes saved coworkers and their shifts.
  • "Sent" means sent: the app confirms only after the server saved the request. On a bad connection it says "We couldn't send your request. Please try again."

Design decisions

A quiet link, not a big button. Working the shift is the main action. The link sits under the details, and its text matches what the client switched on.

One sheet, one step. What to do and why on one screen. Swap adds two short steps in the same sheet.

"You're still scheduled" on every screen. A request is never mistaken for a yes.

Easy to change your mind. Re-apply for a passed-on offer, ask again after a declined swap, and confirm before anything final.

Details before commitment. Every request opens the shift's place, time and the staffer's own pay.

Managers see what needs them first. Warnings for stale and urgent requests, bulk approve with reasons, history in plain words.

How I worked

  • Wrote prototype specs and turned them into mockups with AI, covering readable fonts, contrast and web, iOS and Android variants
  • Agreed on the flows with the team before coding, then built the screens in Vue 3 and TypeScript
  • Built the backend in PHP with PostgreSQL: who can see and take a shift, time limits per client, all-or-nothing swaps, and the history log
  • Wrote Vitest unit tests and Playwright end-to-end tests for every feature
  • Worked with an AI-assisted workflow in Claude Code, reviewing every change before it shipped
  • Wrote the client overview in plain words, with screenshots of every step
